Open-source screen recorder and async video platform with bring-your-own storage

A Chrome extension records screen, tab or webcam and uploads to an API that uses storage the owner chooses; a web app handles library and sharing.

Sector

Category

Individuals and teams who want an open-source alternative to hosted screen-recording tools, with the option to keep recordings in their own storage.

What it looks like in use

The small panel in the browser toolbar before and after a recording, and the web library where recordings are kept.

From one click to a recording link

  1. Sam picks what to record

    He clicks the extension in his browser and chooses this tab, the whole screen or just the webcam.

  2. He talks while it records

    The microphone is on, and a small camera bubble can sit on screen while he shows his work. Drawing tools are available on the page.

  3. Stopping uploads it to his account

    The video is sent to his account and stored in his own bucket if he has connected one, otherwise in managed storage.

  4. A link to the recording is ready

    When the upload finishes, he gets a share link and can open the recording in the web app, where he can trim it or set who may watch.

  5. Every recording lives in one library

    All his videos sit in one library in the web app, which he can share with teammates.

01

The challenge

Hosted screen-recording tools put every video on someone else's infrastructure. Teams that record walkthroughs, demos and bug reports often want to choose where the files live, including a bucket in a specific region for data-residency reasons.

Browser extensions also face real platform limits. The browser's extension rules do not let a background script keep a recorder alive, capture APIs differ between screen and tab, and region capture has no native API.

02

The solution

A Chrome extension handles capture and recording and uploads as the signed-in user. The backend stores video through a pluggable storage provider (local disk by default for development, or common cloud storage and video services, including Google Drive; managed or bring-your-own) and keeps metadata in PostgreSQL.

A Next.js web app provides the library, player, editor and sharing. Access is controlled: listing and managing recordings requires a signed-in account, streaming requires a short-lived signed playback session, and recording share links follow each recording's share settings, either anyone with the link or restricted to signed-in people it is shared with. The project is explicit about trade-offs: region recording is documented as deferred with a researched approach.

05

Implementation notes

Engineering decisions worth knowing about.

  1. Offscreen recording

    An offscreen document holds the recorder for the whole session because MV3 service workers are short-lived.

  2. Typed messaging

    A message-typed protocol connects the popup, background, offscreen and content scripts.

  3. Pluggable storage

    A storage factory picks a provider per account: local disk, S3-compatible storage, Google Drive, Firebase, Cloudinary, Mux or Bunny. Metadata lives in PostgreSQL with Alembic migrations.

  4. Access control

    Listing and managing recordings needs a signed-in account (JWT). Streaming needs a short-lived signed playback session and signed video tokens. Recording share links follow each recording's share settings: anyone with the link, or restricted to signed-in people it is shared with.

  5. Teams and tiers

    Roles, teams and tier limits (recording length, video count, managed storage) are defined in the backend configuration, with Stripe for billing.

  6. Tests

    The backend has a tests folder covering areas such as playback sessions, storage, tiers and the video editor, and the web app has a Vitest setup. Tests exist; they have not been run for this write-up.

  7. Store readiness

    Privacy and store listing documents are prepared for extension submission.
